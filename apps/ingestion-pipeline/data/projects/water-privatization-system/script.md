# Script — NARRATOR Voice

**The Water Cartels: How Privatization Turned a Human Right Into a Commodity**

*Voice: GatorSquare Investigator. Institutional weight. Vivid. Conversational. Every sentence earns the next.*

---

**PANEL 01 — THE TAP**

NARRATOR: She turns the tap. Nothing.

It's January 2000 in Cochabamba, Bolivia. Population six hundred thousand. Average income: two dollars a day. Last month, her water bill was manageable. This month, it's twenty dollars. A third of her family's income. For water.

Something changed. Not the pipes. Not the reservoir. The ownership.

---

**PANEL 02 — THE SIGNATURE**

NARRATOR: Three months earlier, in a government office nobody photographed, Bolivian officials signed a forty-year concession agreement. The other party: Aguas del Tunari — a consortium controlled by Bechtel Corporation. A San Francisco company worth seventeen point six billion dollars.

The deal wasn't Bolivia's idea. The World Bank offered a loan. The loan had a condition. Condition 4.3: privatize the water supply. No public subsidy.

Bolivia needed the loan. Bechtel wanted the contract. The World Bank connected them. Everyone at the table got what they wanted.

Everyone at the table.

---

**PANEL 03 — THE PRICE BOARD**

NARRATOR: The new prices went up on a board outside the utility office. Old rates crossed out. New rates written below in marker. Two hundred to three hundred percent increase. Overnight.

People stood in line and did the math. Some did it on their hands. A family of five earning sixty dollars a month was now being charged twenty dollars for water alone.

The math doesn't work. It was never supposed to work. Not for them.

---

**PANEL 04 — THE MARCH**

NARRATOR: They didn't file a complaint. They didn't write a letter. They marched.

La Coordinadora — a coalition of farmers, factory workers, teachers, and grandmothers — called a general strike. Tens of thousands filled Cochabamba's central plaza. The signs were hand-painted because nobody could afford printed ones. "El agua es nuestra." The water is ours.

Bechtel's local manager watched from behind a window.

---

**PANEL 05 — THE SNIPER**

NARRATOR: On April 8, 2000, the Bolivian government declared a state of siege. Military snipers took positions on rooftops above the protest.

Victor Hugo Daza was seventeen. He was not a protester. He was walking past. A sniper shot him through the face. He died on the cobblestones of a street named after Simon Bolivar — the liberator.

One boy. One bullet. Fired on behalf of a water company headquartered four thousand miles away.

---

**PANEL 06 — THE BOARDROOM**

NARRATOR: To understand how a boy dies for water in Bolivia, you have to go to a conference room in Washington, D.C.

World Bank headquarters. Eighteen hundred H Street. The room is temperature-controlled. The water is complimentary.

On the screen: "Water Sector Reform — Structural Adjustment Requirements." The phrase "private sector participation" appears seven times in a twelve-page document. The word "community" appears once. The word "profit" does not appear at all. It doesn't need to. Everyone in the room already understands.

---

**PANEL 07 — THE REVOLVING DOOR**

NARRATOR: The door between the World Bank and the water industry doesn't revolve. It doesn't need to. It's always open.

Senior staff move between Bank positions, corporate boards, and government advisory roles. The person writing the policy, the person bidding on the contract, and the person approving the contract have often held each other's jobs.

This is not corruption. Corruption is hidden. This is organizational architecture. It's on LinkedIn.

---

**PANEL 08 — THE GLOBAL MAP**

NARRATOR: Cochabamba was not unique. It was the loudest.

Manila. Jakarta. Buenos Aires. Dar es Salaam. Johannesburg. Atlanta. Dozens of cities. Same pattern. World Bank loan. Privatization condition. Foreign corporation. Price hike.

Some cities fought back. Some are still fighting. At least two hundred and thirty-five cities in thirty-seven countries have re-municipalized their water since 2000. Including Paris — home city of Suez and Veolia.

The French companies that sold privatization to the developing world couldn't sell it to their own capital.

---

**PANEL 09 — THE CONTRACT**

NARRATOR: Here's what a concession agreement looks like when you read the fine print.

Thirty-year exclusive operation rights. Guaranteed minimum revenue — meaning the government pays the corporation if customers can't. Currency fluctuation protection — meaning the government absorbs exchange rate risk. And the crown jewel: the right to international arbitration under bilateral investment treaties.

A corporation can sue a sovereign nation for changing water policy. The nation cannot sue the corporation for failing to deliver water.

Read that again.

---

**PANEL 10 — THE DISCONNECTION**

NARRATOR: Manila, 2003. A utility worker turns a valve. A family loses water. They're two months behind on payments.

Suez took over Manila's eastern water zone in 1997. Over the next decade, prices rose five hundred percent. Service didn't improve proportionally. But disconnection rates did.

The mother holds an empty bucket. Behind her, three children. The youngest doesn't understand what's happening. The oldest does.

---

**PANEL 11 — THE PIPELINE**

NARRATOR: Same city. Same utility. Two pipelines.

One flows clean, pressurized water to a neighborhood where people can pay. The other — older, leaking, corroded — delivers intermittent brown water to a neighborhood where people cannot.

This is not neglect. This is triage by income. The system is not broken. It is sorting.

---

**PANEL 12 — THE CYCLE DIAGRAM**

NARRATOR: Here's the cycle. It has six stages. It has been running for thirty years.

One: A public utility exists. Imperfect but accessible. Two: The government underfunds it — sometimes by choice, sometimes under IMF austerity. Three: Service deteriorates. The deterioration becomes the argument: "The public sector has failed." Four: A corporation takes over. Prices rise to cover costs plus profit plus executive pay plus shareholder returns. Five: People who can't pay lose water. They protest. Six: Under pressure, the contract is partially reversed.

Then stage one restarts. Except now the infrastructure is degraded, the institutional knowledge is gone, and the community has spent years without reliable water.

The wheel keeps turning. The water keeps flowing — to those who can afford it.

---

**PANEL 13 — THE QUARTERLY REPORT**

NARRATOR: Two documents. Same resource.

Veolia's annual report: water division revenue, eleven point four billion euros. Growth target: seven percent. Shareholder return: adequate.

UNICEF report, same year: eight hundred twenty-nine thousand deaths annually from waterborne disease. Two hundred ninety-seven thousand of them children under five.

The financial document uses the word "opportunity." The health document uses the word "crisis." They're describing the same water.

---

**PANEL 14 — THE LAWSUIT**

NARRATOR: After Bechtel was expelled from Cochabamba, it sued Bolivia for twenty-five million dollars. Lost future profits.

The case was filed at ICSID — the International Centre for Settlement of Investment Disputes. ICSID is housed inside the World Bank.

Follow the chain: the World Bank required privatization. Bechtel got the contract. Bolivia cancelled it. Bechtel sued at the World Bank's own court.

The institution that created the problem is adjudicating the complaint about the solution. This is not irony. It's infrastructure.

---

**PANEL 15 — THE COUNTER-MOVEMENT**

NARRATOR: July 28, 2010. The United Nations General Assembly passes Resolution 64/292. One hundred twenty-two votes in favor. None against. Forty-one abstentions.

Water is recognized as a human right.

There is applause. There are photographs. There is a press release.

There is no enforcement mechanism. The resolution is non-binding. It is, in legal terms, a suggestion.

Corporations have enforceable property rights. Citizens have aspirational human rights. The asymmetry is not a bug. It is the operating system.

---

**PANEL 16 — THE BRABECK CLIP**

NARRATOR: In 2005, the CEO of Nestlé — the world's largest food and water company — gave an interview.

Peter Brabeck-Letmathe, sitting in a leather chair, said: "The one opinion, which I think is extreme, is represented by the NGOs, who bang on about declaring water a public right. Water is a foodstuff, like any other, and like any other foodstuff it should have a market value."

He was not being provocative. He was being precise. He was describing the system exactly as it operates.

---

**PANEL 17 — THE EXTRACTION**

NARRATOR: Mecosta County, Michigan. Population: eleven thousand. Nestlé operates a pumping station here. Four hundred gallons per minute. Extracted from the aquifer. The permit costs two hundred dollars per year.

Nestlé bottles this water and sells it for a dollar fifty per half-liter. The markup is roughly two thousand times. The creek nearby has gone dry. The aquifer level has measurably dropped.

When the community objected, Nestlé's legal team argued that groundwater is a private property right, not a public resource.

Two hundred dollars for the permit. Millions in profit. Zero accountability for the aquifer.

---

**PANEL 18 — THE LEAD PIPE**

NARRATOR: Flint, Michigan. 2014. The state government, under emergency financial management, switched the city's water source from Lake Huron to the Flint River. The savings: five million dollars over two years.

The Flint River was corrosive. The city's pipes were old. Many were lead. The corrosive water dissolved the lead into the drinking supply. One hundred thousand residents were poisoned. Twelve died of Legionnaires' disease. The lead levels in some homes were twice the EPA threshold for hazardous waste.

The population of Flint is fifty-four percent Black. The median household income is twenty-eight thousand dollars.

This was not an accident. It was arithmetic. Someone calculated that these people's health was worth less than five million dollars. And they were right — because nobody stopped them.

---

**PANEL 19 — THE QUEUE**

NARRATOR: Cape Town, South Africa. 2018. Countdown clocks on every news channel. "Day Zero" — the day the taps go off entirely.

Residents queue with plastic containers at municipal water points. Twenty-five liters per person. Armed guards monitor the line. In the wealthy suburbs, private boreholes tap into aquifers below the city. Different water source. Same drought.

The crisis was blamed on climate. But Cape Town's water infrastructure had been underfunded for decades. The poor, predominantly Black communities of the Cape Flats had been living in water poverty long before the middle class noticed.

Day Zero was new for some. It was Tuesday for others.

---

**PANEL 20 — THE TRADING FLOOR**

NARRATOR: December 2020. The Chicago Mercantile Exchange. A new financial product begins trading.

Water futures. Ticker symbol: NQH2O. Indexed to the Nasdaq Veles California Water Index.

For the first time in human history, water is a tradeable financial commodity. Alongside oil. Alongside gold. Alongside pork bellies.

Speculators can now place bets on water scarcity. The drier the forecast, the higher the price, the greater the profit. Someone makes money every time a reservoir drops.

---

**PANEL 21 — THE AQUIFER**

NARRATOR: The Ogallala Aquifer sits beneath the American Great Plains. Eight states. One hundred seventy-four thousand square miles. It irrigates twenty billion dollars of agriculture annually.

It took millions of years to fill. It is being emptied in decades. The extraction rate exceeds the recharge rate by a factor of six to one in some areas. Parts of the Ogallala will be functionally dry within thirty years.

When the water runs out, the farms stop. When the farms stop, the food chain breaks. When the food chain breaks, everything downstream breaks with it.

This is not a future scenario. This is current hydrology.

---

**PANEL 22 — THE STRESS MAP**

NARRATOR: A projection map, 2040. Fifteen years from now.

Red: extremely high water stress. It covers the Middle East, North Africa, South Asia, parts of Central America. Thirty-three countries. Two billion people.

Orange: high water stress. Southern Europe, northern China, the American Southwest.

The map doesn't show political boundaries. Water doesn't respect them. The Colorado River serves forty million Americans. It no longer reaches the sea.

---

**PANEL 23 — THE TWO GLASSES**

NARRATOR: Two glasses of water.

The first: crystal clear, Nestlé label, two dollars and fifty cents. Sourced from a Michigan aquifer. Sold at a convenience store.

The second: brown, cloudy, from a communal tap in a low-income district. Technically free. Contains trace lead, arsenic, and bacterial contamination.

Both are "access to water." Only one is access to CLEAN water. The human right doesn't specify which.

---

**PANEL 24 — THE CHILD**

NARRATOR: A child drinks from a mud puddle.

Every two minutes, a child under five dies from waterborne disease. Two hundred ninety-seven thousand per year. More than malaria. More than measles. More than conflict.

This is not a market failure. The market is working exactly as designed. Markets allocate scarce resources to those who can pay the most. That is what markets do. The question is whether a substance required for human survival should be subject to market allocation.

That question has been answered. By Bechtel. By Suez. By Veolia. By Nestlé. By the World Bank. By the Chicago Mercantile Exchange.

Their answer is yes.

---

**PANEL 25 — THE QUESTION**

NARRATOR: A dam. Half in shadow, half in light.

On the shadow side: Suez. Veolia. Nestlé. Bechtel. Wall Street water futures. Bilateral investment treaties. The World Bank. The revolving door.

On the light side: Cochabamba's La Coordinadora. Paris re-municipalization. UN Resolution 64/292. Community water cooperatives. Indigenous water protectors. The two hundred and thirty-five cities that took their water back.

The question is not whether water will be the defining resource conflict of the twenty-first century. It already is.

The question is who will control it: the communities who need it, or the corporations who can profit from it.

News reported the Cochabamba Water War.

GatorSquare investigated the system that produced it.
