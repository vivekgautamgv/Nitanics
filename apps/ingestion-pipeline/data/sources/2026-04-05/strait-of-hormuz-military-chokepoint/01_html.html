Act I — The Gap

Panel 1: The Two Numbers

[Stage: Act I — The Gap]

Twenty-one miles. Twenty million barrels.

The Strait of Hormuz — the narrow passage between Iran and Oman that connects the Persian Gulf to the open ocean — is 21 nautical miles wide at its narrowest point. On a clear day, you can see across it. A fast boat can cross it in under an hour. A swimmer with ambition and poor judgment could attempt it.

Through this gap flows 20.5 million barrels of oil every single day. One-fifth of global consumption. Saudi crude. Iraqi crude. Kuwaiti, Emirati, Qatari exports. The fuel that runs Japan’s factories, heats South Korea’s cities, feeds China’s refineries, and moves India’s trucks. One-fifth of the energy that powers the global economy, funneled through a waterway narrower than the English Channel.

Those two numbers should not coexist. And for forty years, defense planners have known that eventually, they wouldn’t.

Panel 2: The Corridor

[Stage: Act I — The Gap]

The navigable channel is far narrower than the strait itself.

Two shipping lanes, each two nautical miles wide — one inbound, one outbound — separated by a two-mile buffer zone. Six miles total. Through this corridor, approximately 100 vessels transit every day: supertankers carrying two million barrels each, LNG carriers hauling Qatari natural gas worth $80 million per cargo, container ships, bulk carriers, naval vessels.

The shipping lanes run primarily through Omani territorial waters, with a portion crossing into Iranian waters. They are governed by the United Nations Convention on the Law of the Sea — UNCLOS — which guarantees the right of transit passage through international straits. Iran signed but never ratified it. Oman did. The legal architecture governing the world’s most important shipping corridor is, at best, contested.

Six miles of navigable water. One hundred ships a day. No alternative route. The entire Persian Gulf has one exit, and this is it.

Panel 3: The Dependents

[Stage: Act I — The Gap]

China absorbs 37.7% of all oil flowing through the strait — more than any other nation. India takes 14.7%. South Korea: 12%. Japan: 10.9%.

But the raw numbers disguise the real vulnerability. China imports 40% of its oil through Hormuz, but fossil fuels account for only 20% of China’s total energy. China can absorb a shock. Japan cannot. Eighty-seven percent of Japan’s total energy consumption comes from imported fossil fuels. Ninety percent of its crude oil originates in the Middle East, and virtually all of it transits the Strait of Hormuz. South Korea is nearly identical: 81% import dependence, 70% of crude from the Middle East, more than 95% routed through the strait.

The world’s third and fourth largest economies run on a supply line that passes through a 21-mile gap controlled, on its northern shore, by a country that has threatened to close it at least a dozen times since 1979.

Japan and South Korea hold strategic reserves — roughly 140 days and 90 days respectively. Reserves are buffers. They are not solutions.

Panel 4: The Memory

[Stage: Act I — The Gap]

On April 14, 1988, the guided-missile frigate USS Samuel B. Roberts was escorting reflagged Kuwaiti oil tankers through the Persian Gulf — part of Operation Earnest Will, the largest naval convoy operation since World War II — when it struck an Iranian M-08 contact mine.

The explosion ripped a 15-foot hole in the hull and broke the ship’s keel. The crew fought for hours to save the vessel. No one died. When US Navy divers recovered other mines from the area, the serial numbers matched mines seized from an Iranian barge seven months earlier.

Four days later, the United States launched Operation Praying Mantis — the largest American naval engagement since World War II. In a single afternoon, US forces sank the Iranian frigate Sahand, disabled the frigate Sabalan, destroyed the fast-attack craft Joshan, and demolished two oil platforms the IRGC had been using as forward staging bases.

Iran lost half its operational navy in eight hours. The lesson Washington took: overwhelming force works. The lesson Tehran took was different: never fight the Americans on their terms. Adapt. Go asymmetric. Make the strait itself the weapon.

Panel 5: The Rehearsals

[Stage: Act I — The Gap]

Thirty-one years later, Iran tested the system again. Not with a navy. With precision.

May 2019: four commercial ships — two Saudi tankers, one Norwegian, one Emirati — were sabotaged in the waters off Fujairah, the UAE’s major oil storage hub on the Gulf of Oman side of the strait. Explosive charges below the waterline. No sinkings, but the message was unmistakable.

June 2019: two tankers attacked in the Gulf of Oman with limpet mines. The Kokuka Courageous and the Front Altair. The US Navy released infrared video of an IRGC patrol boat retrieving an unexploded mine from the Kokuka Courageous’s hull.

September 2019: eighteen drones and seven cruise missiles struck Saudi Aramco’s oil processing facilities at Abqaiq and Khurais — the largest oil processing complex on earth. 5.7 million barrels per day knocked offline in a single strike. Half of Saudi Arabia’s output. Five percent of global supply. Oil spiked 15% in a single trading session.

The Houthis in Yemen claimed Abqaiq. The United States, Britain, France, and Germany attributed it to Iran. None of these incidents closed the strait. They were not meant to. They were reconnaissance — mapping the system’s pressure points, testing how insurance markets responded, measuring how quickly prices moved, observing what the US military did and did not do. The IRGC was studying the machine before it decided to break it.

Act II — The Arsenal

Panel 6: The Two Navies

[Stage: Act II — The Arsenal]

Iran does not have a navy. It has two.

The IRIN — the Islamic Republic of Iran Navy — operates conventional warships: frigates, corvettes, submarines. It conducts port visits and flag-showing exercises. It is a navy that exists, in large part, for diplomatic symbolism.

The IRGCN — the Islamic Revolutionary Guard Corps Navy — is something else entirely. Based at Bandar Abbas, the port city that sits directly on the northern shore of the Strait of Hormuz, the IRGCN operates more than 1,500 fast-attack craft. Small, fast, expendable. The newest platforms — the Heydar-110 class — reach speeds of 110 knots, armed with anti-ship cruise missiles capable of striking targets 300 to 750 kilometers away.

Their doctrine is called swarm warfare. Dozens of fast boats converging simultaneously on a single target from multiple directions, each carrying missiles or torpedoes, each expendable, each a threat that a warship’s defense systems must track and engage individually. In the open ocean, a carrier strike group can handle swarms. In a 21-mile-wide strait with civilian traffic, coastal geography, and overlapping radar returns, the calculus changes.

The IRGCN does not need to defeat the US Fifth Fleet. It needs to make the strait too dangerous to transit.

Panel 7: The Invisible Weapon

[Stage: Act II — The Arsenal]

Iran’s most dangerous weapons cost less than a used car.

The IRGC maintains an arsenal estimated at 2,000 to 5,000 naval mines — a mix of Soviet-era contact mines, Chinese EM-52 rocket-propelled rising mines, and domestically manufactured variants. Some are sophisticated. Most are simple. All of them work.

Here is the asymmetry that defines the strait: a naval mine costs a few thousand dollars. The supertanker it can destroy costs $150 million. The cargo it carries is worth $200 million. The insurance claim cascades through Lloyd’s of London. The shipping lane closes for weeks while minesweepers — slow, specialized, vulnerable ships — clear the area meter by meter.

And the critical fact that keeps admirals awake: mines can be laid from fishing boats. From dhows — the traditional wooden sailing vessels that have worked these waters for centuries. From any small craft among the thousands that operate in and around the strait every day. A carrier strike group cannot inspect every fishing boat in the Persian Gulf. It cannot distinguish a mine-layer from a fisherman until the mines are already in the water.

Mine clearance is the most time-consuming operation in naval warfare. Laying them takes hours. Clearing them takes weeks. The math is entirely in Iran’s favor.

Panel 8: The Coastal Wall

[Stage: Act II — The Arsenal]

The Iranian coastline along the strait bristles with anti-ship cruise missiles.

Noor. Qader. Khalij-e-Fars — a ballistic anti-ship missile specifically designed to hit moving vessels. All mounted on mobile truck launchers. All capable of firing from behind the ridgelines and sand dunes that line Iran’s southern coast. All relocatable within minutes of firing.

Their range covers the entire strait and extends deep into the Gulf of Oman — meaning even ships that have already transited are not safe. A missile launched from a hillside near Bandar Abbas can reach a tanker off the coast of Fujairah. The firing position that existed five minutes ago is empty by the time the counterstrike arrives.

Air power can degrade these batteries. It did — Operation Epic Fury struck coastal installations along the entire littoral. But “degraded” is not “eliminated.” The coastline is 1,500 kilometers long. The launchers are mobile. The terrain offers natural concealment. The question is never whether all the missiles have been destroyed. The question is whether enough survive to threaten a single tanker. And the answer, for the foreseeable future, is yes.

Panel 9: The Doctrine

[Stage: Act II — The Arsenal]

The IRGC studied its own defeat.

After Operation Praying Mantis in 1988, Iran’s military establishment conducted an extensive internal review. The conclusion was not to build a bigger navy. It was to build a different kind of force — one that turned the strait’s geography from a vulnerability into a weapon.

Conventional naval warfare in a confined waterway favors the defender, not the attacker. A 21-mile-wide strait is too narrow for a carrier to maneuver freely, too shallow for submarines to operate safely, too cluttered with civilian traffic for weapons systems designed for open-ocean warfare. The IRGC redesigned its entire maritime doctrine around this insight.

The result: a force optimized not for naval battle but for area denial. Make the strait too costly to use. You do not need to sink an aircraft carrier. You need to sink one tanker. One burning supertanker, leaking crude into the shipping lane, visible on satellite imagery worldwide — that image closes the strait more effectively than a thousand missiles. Because the closure is not physical. It is actuarial. It is the insurance underwriters in London who decide, based on that image, that the risk is unacceptable.

The IRGC understood before anyone else that the strait’s real gatekeepers sit in Lloyd’s, not the Pentagon.

Panel 10: The Trigger

[Stage: Act II — The Arsenal]

February 28, 2026. Operation Epic Fury begins.

US and Israeli strikes hit Iran across dozens of targets. The IRGC Navy was a primary objective. By Day 16, CENTCOM reported 130 Iranian naval vessels hit, more than 100 destroyed, every Soleimani-class corvette — the IRGCN’s newest and most capable surface combatants — sunk. Official assessment: “combat ineffective.”

The world’s press reported the destruction as a victory. In a conventional sense, it was. The IRGC’s surface fleet no longer existed. Fast-attack boats that had trained for decades to swarm American warships were burning at their piers or resting on the seafloor.

But the IRGC had already activated the other playbook. The asymmetric one. The one that does not require ships. The coastal missiles were firing before the navy was gone. The mine-laying boats — small, indistinguishable from civilian traffic — were already at work. And the most powerful weapon of all was not military. It was the phone call to Lloyd’s.

Act III — The Invisible Siege

Panel 11: The Declaration

[Stage: Act III — The Invisible Siege]

March 11, 2026. A spokesperson for the IRGC’s Khatam al-Anbiya Central Headquarters — the operational command that coordinates Iran’s most sensitive military activities — issued a statement.

“Not a litre of oil will get through the Strait of Hormuz.”

Any vessel linked to the United States, Israel, or their allies would be considered a legitimate target. Expect oil at $200 per barrel.

This was not the first time Iran had made the threat. It was the first time the military context made it credible. Iranian forces had been struck. Iranian territory had been bombed. Iranian commanders had been killed. The restraint that had held for forty years — the calculation that closing the strait would invite American retaliation — was moot. Retaliation was already underway. The IRGC had nothing left to lose by executing the threat it had rehearsed for a generation.

Panel 12: The Underwriters

[Stage: Act III — The Invisible Siege]

The real closure began not with a mine but with a spreadsheet.

In the Lloyd’s building in London — a marketplace that has underwritten maritime risk since 1688, that insured ships during the Napoleonic Wars and both World Wars — war risk premiums for the Persian Gulf quadrupled overnight. From 0.25% to 1% of a vessel’s hull replacement value, renewable every seven days. For a supertanker valued at $150 million, that meant $1.5 million per transit. Per week.

Then the P&I clubs — the mutual insurance associations that cover third-party liability for shipowners — made the decisive move. Gard, Skuld, North-Standard, the London P&I Club, Steamship Mutual: all cancelled war risk extensions for vessels entering the Persian Gulf. These are not boutique insurers. They collectively cover the majority of global merchant shipping tonnage.

Without P&I coverage, a commercial vessel cannot legally operate. No port will accept it. No cargo owner will load it. No bank will finance it. The coverage withdrawal did what no missile could: it made the strait commercially nonexistent.

Lloyd’s writes 70 to 80 percent of the world’s war risk business. When Lloyd’s prices a waterway as uninsurable, it ceases to function as a trade route — regardless of whether any ship has actually been struck.

Panel 13: The Selective Weapon

[Stage: Act III — The Invisible Siege]

The insurance market did something no navy could: it discriminated by flag.

Ships with a perceived American, British, or Israeli connection — ownership, flag, management, cargo destination — were charged three times the standard war risk premium. Chinese vessels, Indian vessels, and ships from non-aligned nations received different treatment. Lower premiums. In some cases, Iranian guarantees of safe passage.

This was not a market anomaly. It was a geopolitical strategy executed through actuarial tables. Iran could not militarily distinguish between a Chinese tanker and an American one in a shooting war. But insurance markets could. And by creating a two-tier risk environment, the IRGC achieved something remarkable: it used the Western financial system’s own risk-pricing mechanism to fracture the coalition against it.

China, which absorbs 37.7% of Hormuz oil flows, suddenly had an incentive to maintain quiet channels with Tehran. India, at 14.7%, had the same. The strait was not closed to everyone equally. It was closed selectively — and the selection criteria tracked Iran’s diplomatic interests with precision.

Panel 14: The Mines Go In

[Stage: Act III — The Invisible Siege]

March 10. American intelligence confirmed what satellite imagery had already suggested: Iran had begun laying mines in the Strait of Hormuz.

The initial count was modest — a few dozen. But a few dozen is enough. A single mine in a shipping lane forces every vessel to stop until the lane is verified clear. Mine clearance requires dedicated minesweepers — specialized ships that move slowly, methodically, and are themselves vulnerable to the weapons they are trying to remove.

On March 11, US forces sank 16 Iranian mine-laying vessels. Pentagon briefings presented this as progress. It was arithmetic failure. Iran retained 80 to 90 percent of its small boat fleet. The thousands of fishing boats and dhows operating in the strait — vessels that are physically indistinguishable from mine-layers until the moment a mine rolls off the stern — remained untouched. Because they are untouchable. You cannot sink the Persian Gulf’s entire fishing fleet on suspicion.

Mines can be laid in darkness, in rough weather, from vessels that draw no attention. The Samuel B. Roberts hit a mine in 1988 that had been laid by an Iranian barge among dozens of similar vessels. Thirty-eight years later, the same tactic remained viable. The technology is ancient. The strategic logic is eternal.

Panel 15: The Rate Shock

[Stage: Act III — The Invisible Siege]

Supertanker charter rates — the daily cost of renting a Very Large Crude Carrier — went from approximately $200,000 per day to nearly $800,000 per day within a single week. A fourfold increase.

Each VLCC voyage through the strait now carried an additional $10 to $14 million in war risk premiums alone. That cost does not disappear. It propagates. The charterer passes it to the refiner. The refiner passes it to the distributor. The distributor passes it to the gas station, the airline, the trucking company, the factory. Every link in the chain adds a margin for uncertainty, a buffer for risk it cannot predict. By the time the premium reaches a consumer filling their tank in Tokyo or Mumbai, it has been multiplied at every handoff.

The oil is not more expensive because there is less of it — not yet. In the first week, oil already on the water continues to its destination. The floating inventory — tankers already in transit — provides a buffer of two to three weeks. The price spike is pure information: the market pricing in the certainty that supply will fall and the uncertainty of when it will return.

Fear is more expensive than scarcity. Scarcity is calculable. Fear is not.

Panel 16: The Clock Starts

[Stage: Act III — The Invisible Siege]

March 8: Brent crude crosses $100 per barrel. The first time in four years.

By mid-March: $126. A $40 geopolitical risk premium — meaning the market had priced $40 of pure fear above what supply and demand fundamentals would dictate. Forty dollars per barrel of uncertainty, multiplied across 100 million barrels consumed globally every day. Four billion dollars a day in risk premium alone, extracted from the global economy by a 21-mile waterway.

Goldman Sachs modeled scenarios. If the strait reopens within weeks: Brent averages $98 for March-April. If it doesn’t: $140-plus sustained, with $200 in a worst case. The $200 figure matched the IRGC’s own declaration — an unusual convergence between a Wall Street investment bank and a Revolutionary Guard spokesman.

The Dallas Federal Reserve published its assessment in the measured language of central bankers: the world was entering “a period of higher inflation and reduced manufacturing.” Translation: stagflation. The worst macroeconomic outcome — rising prices and falling output simultaneously. The condition that central banks have no good tool to fight.

Act IV — The Cascade

Panel 17: Hour One

[Stage: Act IV — The Cascade]

The Automatic Identification System — AIS, the satellite-tracked transponder that every commercial vessel is required to carry — told the story in real time.

On screens at maritime monitoring centers worldwide, the strait went dark. Ship icons that had been moving through the corridor in orderly lines — inbound, outbound, spaced like cars on a highway — began stopping. Turning. Some switched off their transponders entirely, going dark in contested waters.

Algorithmic trading systems detected the pattern within minutes. Oil futures spiked before any human analyst had written a sentence, before any government had issued a statement. The machines read the AIS data, correlated it with news feeds, and began buying. High-frequency trading algorithms designed to exploit information asymmetries by fractions of a second had determined, before most naval commanders were briefed, that 20% of global oil supply had just stopped flowing.

The first casualty of a chokepoint closure is not a ship. It is the price.

Panel 18: Day Three

[Stage: Act IV — The Cascade]

Here is what $126 oil means to people who will never hear a siren or see a warship.

A hospital in Dhaka, Bangladesh runs on diesel backup generators during the country’s frequent power cuts. Those generators keep premature infants alive in the neonatal ward. The diesel now costs 30% more. The hospital’s budget was already stretched. Something will be cut. It will not be reported as a casualty of the Strait of Hormuz.

A trucking company in Indiana adds a fuel surcharge to every delivery. The surcharge is passed to grocery stores, restaurants, construction sites. The price of milk rises by cents. Multiply those cents by every gallon of milk in every store in its network, and the number is billions across the American economy.

A fishing fleet in Accra, Ghana, calculates that fuel costs now exceed the value of the catch. The boats stay in port. The fishermen earn nothing. The market stalls that sell their catch sit empty. The protein gap is filled by nothing.

The war is in the Persian Gulf. The cost is distributed across every economy on earth, falling heaviest on those with the least capacity to absorb it.

Panel 19: The Reserves

[Stage: Act IV — The Cascade]

The International Energy Agency — the organization created in 1974 specifically because the 1973 OPEC embargo nearly toppled Western economies — activated the system it was built for.

Four hundred million barrels released from strategic petroleum reserves across 31 member nations. The largest coordinated release in the IEA’s 52-year history. Larger than the 2011 Libya response. Larger than the 2022 Russia-Ukraine drawdown.

Four hundred million barrels is enormous. It is also 20 days of Hormuz traffic. If the strait stays closed for a month, the reserves buy time. If it stays closed for three months, they buy a slower crisis. If it stays closed for six months, they run out — and the world enters territory that no economic model has ever been tested against.

There is another constraint that strategic reserves cannot solve: time. Oil released from the US Strategic Petroleum Reserve requires approximately 13 days to reach markets after a presidential release order. It must move through pipelines, shipping networks, and refining capacity before becoming gasoline. Even the world’s largest emergency stockpile cannot flood the market with crude on demand. The reserves are a slow weapon deployed against a fast crisis.

Panel 20: The Alternatives That Don’t Work

[Stage: Act IV — The Cascade]

The pipeline options look reassuring on a map. They are insufficient in mathematics.

The Saudi East-West Pipeline — Petroline — runs from the Abqaiq processing center to Yanbu on the Red Sea. Capacity: 5 million barrels per day, expandable to 7 million. Aramco activated it immediately. The UAE’s Abu Dhabi Crude Oil Pipeline carries 1.8 million barrels per day from the Habshan field to Fujairah, bypassing the strait entirely. The Iraq-Turkey pipeline from Kirkuk to Ceyhan on the Mediterranean is theoretically capable of 1.6 million barrels per day, though it has operated intermittently for years due to Kurdish regional disputes and maintenance failures.

Total bypass capacity at maximum output: approximately 8 to 9 million barrels per day. Generous estimate. Against 20 million barrels per day of regular Hormuz flows.

The gap: 11 to 12 million barrels per day that have no bypass. No pipeline, no alternative route, no substitute. Those barrels either flow through the strait or they do not exist in the global market.

The Cape of Good Hope route — sailing around the southern tip of Africa — adds 10 to 14 days to European deliveries, 7 to 10 days to East Asian destinations. It requires more ships, more fuel, and more time, all at quadrupled charter rates. During the 2024 Red Sea crisis caused by Houthi attacks on shipping, container rates doubled from Cape rerouting alone. And that disruption was a fraction of Hormuz’s volume.

No combination of alternatives replaces the strait. The chokepoint has no substitute. That is what makes it a chokepoint.

Panel 21: The Countries That Break First

[Stage: Act IV — The Cascade]

The cascade hits unevenly. The countries with the deepest reserves and most diversified energy sources — the United States, Canada, Norway — absorb the shock. The countries at the end of the longest supply chains, with the smallest buffers and the greatest import dependence, do not.

Japan holds roughly 140 days of strategic petroleum reserves. Adequate for a crisis measured in weeks. But Japan’s reserves are not just oil. They are the only thing standing between the world’s third-largest economy and industrial paralysis. Every day the strait stays closed, that buffer shrinks. Below 90 days, planners begin modeling rationing scenarios. Below 60, they implement them.

South Korea’s buffer is shorter — approximately 90 days. South Korea is the world’s fifth-largest oil importer and one of the largest importers of LNG. Its petrochemical industry — one of the largest globally — depends on Middle Eastern feedstocks. Ninety days is three months. If the strait has not reopened by then, South Korea faces a choice between economic contraction and energy rationing. The choice is political. The consequences are economic.

India, importing 60% of its oil from the Middle East, has less strategic reserve depth and far more population to supply. Bangladesh, Pakistan, the Philippines — nations with minimal reserves and maximal vulnerability — face the most acute impacts with the fewest tools to manage them.

Panel 22: The Fertilizer Thread

[Stage: Act IV — The Cascade]

The cascade has a branch most analysts miss.

One-third of global fertilizer trade transits the Strait of Hormuz. Qatar is one of the world’s largest producers of nitrogen fertilizers, exported as urea and ammonia through the strait. Saudi Arabia, the UAE, and Oman export significant volumes of phosphates and potash.

When the strait closed, nitrogen fertilizer prices at the New Orleans hub — the benchmark for US agricultural markets — rose from $475 per metric ton to $680. A 43% spike. Fertilizer prices do not stay in fertilizer markets. They propagate to food prices with a lag of three to six months — the time between planting season and harvest.

The countries that import the most fertilizer and can least afford price shocks are the same countries that will face food insecurity if the strait stays closed: Egypt, which imports 60% of its wheat. Bangladesh. Sub-Saharan Africa. The Philippines.

The Strait of Hormuz is reported as an energy crisis. It is also, on a six-month delay, a food crisis. The energy cascade and the food cascade are the same cascade, running through the same chokepoint, hitting the same populations — the ones without strategic reserves, without alternative suppliers, without the economic buffers that absorb price shocks before they become humanitarian emergencies.

Act V — The Question

Panel 23: The Pattern

[Stage: Act V — The Question]

Every generation, the system breaks in the same place.

1973: the OPEC embargo cut 5% of supply. Oil prices quadrupled. Western economies entered recession. The response: create the International Energy Agency. Build strategic petroleum reserves. Never again.

1980: the Iran-Iraq War cratered both nations’ production. Global recession. The response: diversify suppliers. Invest in non-OPEC production. Never again.

1987-88: Iran mined the Gulf and attacked tankers. The US launched the largest convoy operation since World War II and destroyed half the Iranian navy. The response: maintain a permanent naval presence in the Gulf. Forward-deploy carrier strike groups. Never again.

2019: Iran probed the system — sabotaging ships, striking Aramco with drones. Oil spiked. The response: absorb it. The system held. No structural changes.

2026: functional closure. Twenty percent of global supply removed. The largest disruption in the history of the oil market. Three to five times larger than any previous crisis in percentage terms.

Each crisis was larger than the last. Each produced institutions designed to prevent the next one. Each institution proved insufficient when the next one arrived. The system learns, but the threats evolve faster than the defenses.

Panel 24: The Invisible Hand

[Stage: Act V — The Question]

The deepest lesson of the 2026 Hormuz crisis will be argued over for decades. But its sharpest insight is already clear.

The strait was not closed by the IRGC’s navy. That navy was destroyed — 130 vessels hit, 100-plus sunk, every major combatant on the seafloor. The strait was not closed by missiles. The coastal batteries were degraded, though not eliminated. The strait was not closed by mines, though mines were laid and mines are terrifying.

The strait was closed by insurance underwriters in London.

Lloyd’s syndicates. P&I mutual associations. Reinsurance companies in Zurich and Munich. The invisible infrastructure of global trade — the system of risk assessment and premium pricing that determines whether a ship sails — decided the Strait of Hormuz was uninsurable. And when the world’s maritime insurance market declares a waterway uninsurable, it ceases to function as a trade route.

The IRGC did not need to close the strait with weapons. It needed to create enough uncertainty — enough credible threat, enough demonstrated willingness, enough historical precedent — that the insurance market closed it for them. The most powerful navy in the world could not overrule an actuarial calculation.

The invisible hand that Adam Smith described as guiding markets toward efficiency turns out to have a corollary: when risk exceeds a threshold, the invisible hand closes the market entirely. No warship in any fleet can force it open.

Panel 25: The 21 Miles

[Stage: Act V — The Question]

Twenty-one miles wide. Twenty million barrels a day.

Those two numbers will define the next decade of energy security, military strategy, and global economic architecture. They already have.

The system that routes one-fifth of the world’s energy through a corridor visible from shore to shore is not a geographical accident. It is the accumulated result of seven decades of decisions: where to drill, where to build refineries, where to route pipelines, where not to invest in alternatives, which risks to price and which to ignore. Every barrel that flowed through the strait for forty years without interruption reinforced the assumption that it would flow forever. The assumption became infrastructure. The infrastructure became dependency. The dependency became vulnerability.

Hormuz is not the vulnerability. It is the symptom. The vulnerability is a global energy system engineered for efficiency — minimum cost, maximum throughput, just-in-time delivery — with no structural redundancy for its most critical chokepoint. A system designed as if the 21 miles would never close.

The strait will reopen. Insurance markets will reprice. Tankers will resume. The question is not whether global energy will flow through Hormuz again. The question is what happens the next time it stops — and whether the system will have changed at all.

Twenty-one miles. The distance is not the problem. The dependency is.
